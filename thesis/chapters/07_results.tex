\section{Cohort and Data Availability}

\subsection{Scar morphology and inter-patient heterogeneity}

Visual inspection of the full-cohort 3D grid confirmed that all 105 cases produce
geometrically coherent LV reconstructions with identifiable core-zone surfaces. Beyond
confirming quality, the grid reveals the amplitude of scar morphology present in this cohort.
Scar extent ranges from small, focal lesions occupying a single wall segment to large,
multi-territory scars covering a substantial fraction of the myocardium. Spatial organisation
varies from compact, contiguous surfaces to fragmented geometries with disconnected
components distributed across different wall regions. Infarction location spans all major
coronary territories---inferior, anterior, lateral, and septal---at basal, mid-ventricular, and
apical levels, with variable wall-depth involvement from subendocardial to near-transmural.
This degree of heterogeneity is a defining characteristic of the cohort and directly motivates
the need for a shape representation that makes no assumptions about scar location, extent,
or connectivity.

\subsection{Clinical metadata availability}

The DEVELOP registry CSV contains records for 173 patients. Of these, 88 carry a
segmentation flag indicating a corresponding ADAS 3D study. This clinical subset does not
fully overlap with the 105 geometrically valid cases identified from the disk scan: identifier
gaps between the two sources mean that some segmented cases lack a clinical record and
vice versa. The operative consequence is that clinical labels can only be linked to geometry
for the cases present in both sources.

Variable completeness within the CSV is highly uneven. MRI-derived scar metrics---LV
mass, border zone, core zone, and channel mass---are the most complete, available for
166--167 patients (96--96.5\%). Demographic and follow-up variables are substantially more
incomplete: sex is recorded for 94 patients and age for 95 out of 173 in the full registry.
However, these figures reflect completeness within the CSV registry alone.
Cross-referencing with the available ADAS 3D segmentations reduces the usable labelled
subset to 48 patients with age data and 48 patients with sex data. Expanding demographic
coverage through registry completion is identified as a priority for future work.

\subsection{Demographic profile}

Among the 48 patients with available demographic labels (out of 105 in the analysis cohort),
38 are male (79.2\%) and 10 are female (20.8\%). This distribution is consistent with the
known epidemiology of myocardial infarction, in which men present roughly a decade earlier
than women, and a predominantly male composition is therefore expected in any post-MI
cohort of this age range.

The mean age of the labelled subset is $63.0 \pm 11.5$ years (median 63.0 years,
IQR 54.8--72.0, range 31--84). The female subgroup shows a slightly lower mean age
($60.3 \pm 11.0$ years) compared to males ($63.7 \pm 11.7$ years), but with only 10 female
patients this difference carries no interpretable signal.

\subsection{Implications for downstream analysis}

The high missingness of demographic and outcome variables---sex available in only 54\% of
the full registry, follow-up outcomes in fewer still---makes any form of supervised clinical
outcome modelling infeasible at the current cohort size. All subsequent geometric analyses therefore proceed on the full 105-case cohort without
clinical sub-grouping.

\section{Polar Map Construction and Orientation Verification}

\subsection{Apex and axis detection}

The multi-candidate apex detection strategy (Chapter~\ref{chap:polarmaps}) was applied
to all 105 cases. LV~PC1 was selected as the apex-defining direction in 74\% of cases,
RV~PC1 in 21\%, and LV~PC2 in the remaining 5\% (Figure~\ref{fig:apexdirection}). In
the RV~PC1 cases, LV~PC1 aligned with circumferential rather than longitudinal variance,
producing an incorrect apex location shifted toward the lateral wall. In the LV~PC2 cases,
near-equal variance in the two largest dimensions caused the true long axis to be captured
by the second rather than the first principal direction. The multi-candidate strategy correctly
identified the apical tip in all 105 cases.

A forced-LV-PC1 ablation confirmed that constraining the axis to LV~PC1 regardless of
eccentricity produces incorrect apex detection in the 25\% of cases where RV~PC1 or
LV~PC2 would otherwise be selected, demonstrating that a single fixed direction is
insufficient for this cohort (Figure~\ref{fig:app_axis_winner} in Appendix). A qualitative
comparison against the reference flattening method of N\'{u}\~{n}ez Garc\'{i}a
\cite{nunez2018} is presented in Section~\ref{sec:polarmapverif}.

\subsection{Per-patient polar map examples}

Figure~\ref{fig:polarmapsteps} illustrates the full projection pipeline for a selection of cases
spanning different scar locations and extents. The five-step sequence proceeds as follows:
the LV (right) and RV (left) are shown in their original three-dimensional orientation; the long
axis is detected and the mesh is re-oriented apex-down; the longitudinal coordinate $s$ is
computed from apex (top) to base (bottom); the circumferential coordinate $\theta$ is
anchored to the septal reference; and the resulting cylindrical coordinates are unrolled into
the two-dimensional bull's-eye grid.

Visual inspection of these examples confirms several properties of the projection. The
apex-to-base gradient is correctly encoded in the radial coordinate, with apical scar
appearing near the centre of the bull's-eye and basal scar at the periphery. The
circumferential position of the scar is preserved: anterior scar maps to the expected angular
sector and the RV insertion points appear at the correct septal position. The binary representation captures the spatial footprint of the scar faithfully; the only
systematic distortion is the compression of the apical region inherent to any cylindrical
projection, where the apex occupies proportionally fewer bins than its true surface area ---
a known trade-off of the bull's-eye representation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:polarmapverif}.

\subsection{Projection method comparison}
\label{sec:projmethods}

Three complementary projection methods were evaluated at the population level: the
vertex-based method (binary scar presence per polar bin), the area-based method
(projected core-zone surface area in mm$^2$ per bin), and the coverage method
(core-zone area divided by total LV area per bin). Population-level superposition maps for
all three methods are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:projmethods}. The spatial pattern of scar
concentration is consistent across methods: all three identify the same high-burden regions
and the same low-burden regions, with differences confined to relative intensity rather than
spatial location.

Segment-level and territory-level Spearman rank correlations between the vertex-based and
area-based metrics across all 105 patients confirm that the binary vertex representation---which
is the primary representation throughout this thesis because of its reversibility and
interpretability---captures equivalent spatial information to the continuous area-weighted
representation. The sole additional insight from the coverage method (CZ area divided by LV surface area
per bin) was the detection of higher local surface density in the RCA territory, obscured in
the vertex-based analysis by that territory's smaller absolute surface area. This does not
contradict the vertex-based territory burden reported in Section~\ref{sec:terrburden}: fewer
RCA bins are affected overall, but those that are carry higher local scar density per bin ---
consistent with RCA infarcts producing focal, concentrated involvement rather than diffuse
spread. Population-level superposition maps for all three methods are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:app_superposition} in the Appendix.

\subsection{Grid resolution sensitivity}

The operational $64 \times 128$ polar grid was compared against a coarser ($32 \times 64$)
and a finer ($128 \times 256$) alternative. The three configurations differ substantially in
their vertex density per bin (Table~\ref{tab:grid}).

    \label{tab:grid}
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
        \toprule
        Grid & Bins & Avg.\ LV vertices/bin \\
        \midrule
        $32 \times 64$ (coarse) & 2\,048 & ${\sim}24$ \\

        $128 \times 256$ (fine) & 32\,768 & ${\sim}1.5$ \\
        \bottomrule

The coarse grid, with ${\sim}24$ vertices per bin, creates a discrete representation in which
scar fragments are easily identified but spatial detail is lost, and adjacent anatomical
structures may be merged into a single bin. The fine grid recovers spatial detail but, with
fewer than 2 vertices per bin on average, many bins are sparsely populated or empty, which
introduces noise into connectivity-based metrics such as fragment counting. The
$64 \times 128$ grid provides the best trade-off: approximately 6 vertices per bin is sufficient
for stable binary scar detection while preserving enough resolution to distinguish all 17 AHA
segments. The spatial pattern of scar concentration in the population mean map is consistent
across all three resolutions (Figure~\ref{fig:app_gridres} in Appendix), confirming that
the reported findings are not an artefact of the chosen grid density.

\subsection{Verification of polar maps}
\label{sec:polarmapverif}

Visual inspection across the four cases shows that scar localisation is broadly consistent
between the two methods in both circumferential and longitudinal coordinates. The primary
discrepancy concerns radial density: the $r^{1/3}$ radial displacement applied by the
reference method \cite{paun2017} concentrates scar representations toward the basal ring,
whereas the linear mapping used here distributes coverage more uniformly along the
apex-to-base axis; as a consequence, the proposed method recovers scar points near the
apex with greater completeness, an advantage most visible in
Figure~\ref{fig:polarmapverif}. More generally, the longitudinal coordinate $s$ of the
proposed method encodes apex-to-base position directly as the normalised projection onto
the long axis, providing a monotonic and geometrically interpretable representation of the
full apex-to-base gradient; the flat disk radius of the reference method depends on the
geometry of the conformal mapping and does not have a simple closed-form relationship
with the normalised apex-to-base position.
Cross-referencing with the 3D LV shell further suggests that the two methods sample
different transmural depths: the reference method labels endocardial surface points in
proximity to the Core Surface and therefore captures the endocardial face of the scar,
whereas the proposed method projects Core Surface vertices directly and therefore
represents the full transmural extent of the scar mass. Taken together, the two approaches
are complementary: differences in radial density and transmural sampling reflect distinct
mapping choices rather than errors in anatomical localisation.

\section{Population-Level Scar Analysis}

All analyses were conducted on vertex-based binary polar maps unless otherwise noted.
The cohort consists of 105 patients; clinical metadata was available for a subset of 48
patients. All inferential tests are non-parametric, justified by the distributional diagnostics
presented first.

\subsection{Normality diagnostics}

Univariate normality was assessed via QQ plots, with the Pearson correlation coefficient
between sample and theoretical normal quantiles compared against the critical values of the
QQ correlation coefficient test at $\alpha = 0.05$. Of all variables examined, only age
satisfied the normality criterion. Since univariate normality is a necessary condition for
multivariate normality, the failure of all scar-related variables already precludes a
multivariate normal joint distribution analytically; a chi-squared QQ plot of the squared
Mahalanobis distances of the full 17-segment burden vector was nonetheless computed to
corroborate this visually.

All scar-related variables showed clear departures from normality. At the AHA segment
level, burden distributions exhibited strong zero-inflation (70--75\% of patients had zero scar
in most segments) and right skewness, with the mean exceeding the median by a factor of
2.4--3.7 and standard-deviation-to-mean ratios of 2--4. Territory-level and global burden
variables showed similar patterns, though with less extreme zero-inflation due to bin
aggregation across segments.

Based on these diagnostics, all subsequent analyses relied on non-parametric methods
(Friedman test, Wilcoxon signed-rank, Spearman rank correlation) and resampling-based
confidence intervals (patient-level bootstrap, 2000 resamples).

\subsection{AHA-17 segment burden and population atlas}

A notable structural property of the segment-level distributions is the dissociation between
prevalence and extent: a segment can be frequently affected yet carry low mean burden, or
rarely affected but carrying high conditional burden when it is. This arises directly from
zero-inflation---the unconditional mean is dragged toward zero even when the conditional
mean among affected patients is substantial. Segment-level summaries therefore report both
quantities separately. For example, the basal inferolateral segment (seg 5) is scar-positive
in 44.8\% of patients yet carries an unconditional mean burden of only 0.032, because the
majority of patients contribute a zero; the apical septal segment (seg 14) is affected in a
similar proportion of patients (41.0\%) but, when involved, carries a conditional mean burden
of 0.190---nearly six times higher.

The Friedman test confirmed that scar burden is non-uniformly distributed across the 17 AHA
segments within patients ($\chi^2 = 115.94$, $p = 3.3 \times 10^{-17}$, $n = 105$). Although
this result is anatomically expected---ischaemic infarction follows coronary territory
boundaries, making uniform distribution implausible a priori---the formal test establishes that
the data are internally consistent with anatomical priors and provides a documented
benchmark for subsequent comparisons. Post-hoc pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with
Bonferroni correction (136 comparisons, adjusted threshold $\alpha = 0.000368$) identified
20 segment pairs with statistically significant burden differences. The significant pairs were
not distributed uniformly across the LV: they were concentrated between the inferior and
inferoseptal mid-ventricular segments (segments 9--11) and the anterior basal segments
(segments 1--4), with mid inferior (segment 10) and mid inferolateral (segment 11) emerging
as the segments most consistently distinguishable from the rest of the wall. No significant
differences were found among segments within the same anatomical ring or between
neighbouring lateral basal segments, indicating that the spatial heterogeneity is structured
rather than diffuse---driven by the contrast between the inferior mid-ventricular wall, where
scar burden is highest, and the anterior basal wall, where it is lowest.

Bootstrap confidence intervals confirm a statistically robust spatial hierarchy. Basal
segments (1--4, mean 0.014--0.023) are clearly and reliably spared: their CI upper bounds
($\leq 0.035$) do not overlap with the CI lower bounds of the mid-cavity hotspot segments
(segs 10--11, CI lower $\geq 0.061$). The mid-inferolateral segment (seg 11, mean = 0.100,
95\% CI [0.070, 0.135]) is the single most affected region, followed by the mid-inferior
(seg 10, mean = 0.088) and apical septal (seg 14, mean = 0.078) segments. The wide CIs
of hotspot segments reflect the high inter-patient variability driven by zero-inflated,
right-skewed distributions. The population atlas identifies a robust inferior-to-apical hotspot
centred on the mid-inferolateral and mid-inferior segments (AHA 11 and 10), straddling the
RCA--LCX boundary, with basal segments consistently spared.

\subsection{Coronary territory burden and archetype maps}
\label{sec:terrburden}

LCX showed the numerically highest mean territory burden (0.055), followed by LAD (0.051)
and RCA (0.047). These differences were not statistically significant (Friedman
$\chi^2 = 3.82$, $p = 0.148$), indicating no systematic coronary territory preference at the
population level. Ring-level analysis showed that the apical and mid-cavity rings carry the
highest scar involvement, with the basal ring consistently spared.

Archetype maps stratified by dominant territory reveal distinct spatial geometries. LAD-dominant
scar is the most diffuse: it extends longitudinally from the anteroseptal segments down through
the apex (segment 17), producing an elongated pattern along the long axis that spans multiple
rings. RCA- and LCX-dominant scar, by contrast, tends toward circumferential spread at the
mid-cavity level, wrapping around the inferior and lateral walls with less longitudinal extension.
This difference reflects the anatomical territory boundaries: the LAD supplies the anterior wall
and apex, while RCA and LCX supply the inferior and lateral walls, which are arranged more
circumferentially in the mid-ventricular ring.

The lower vertex-based RCA burden does not imply geometrically insignificant infarction:
as shown by the coverage analysis (Section~\ref{sec:projmethods}), RCA scar tends to be
locally concentrated when present, with higher surface density per affected bin than LCX or
LAD.

The area-based and vertex-based analyses produced concordant spatial rankings at both
segment and territory levels (Spearman $\rho > 0.85$ across all 17 segments and all three
territories), and both Friedman tests reached the same inferential conclusions, validating
robustness across methodologically independent quantification approaches.

\subsection{Inter-segment correlation structure and PCA}

The $17 \times 17$ Spearman inter-segment correlation matrix reveals a block-diagonal
structure aligned with coronary territories: segments within the same territory co-vary
positively, consistent with shared vascular supply causing contiguous infarction. Importantly,
the dominant within-territory correlation pattern is longitudinal (apex-to-base) rather than
circumferential---vertically adjacent segments (e.g.\ seg 5 and seg 11, both LCX but at
different ring levels) show higher correlations than circumferentially adjacent segments in
different territories, indicating that scar penetrates transmurally along the wall depth rather
than spreading circumferentially. Inter-territory correlations are weak, with the smallest
observed between LCX and RCA ($\rho = 0.114$), supporting the treatment of territory
burdens as approximately independent dimensions.

PCA of the $z$-score standardised 17-segment vectors shows that six components are
needed to explain 72\% of the variance, with PC1 accounting for only 18\%. This low
concentration of variance in the first component indicates that no single spatial mode
dominates scar variability---the scar pattern space is intrinsically high-dimensional, reflecting
the diversity of infarct locations and extents across the cohort.

\subsection{Age and sex associations}

Spearman correlations between age and segment-level burden were computed for the 48
patients with available age data (mean age $63.0 \pm 11.5$ years). No segment reached
significance after Bonferroni correction ($\alpha = 0.05/17 = 0.0029$). The numerically
strongest associations were a positive trend in the mid-inferior segment
(seg 10: $\rho = 0.345$, $p_\text{uncorrected} = 0.016$) and a negative trend at the apex
(seg 17: $\rho = -0.307$, $p_\text{uncorrected} = 0.034$); both became non-significant after
correction ($p_\text{Bonferroni} = 0.280$ and $0.575$, respectively). At the territory level, RCA
showed the numerically highest correlation with age ($\rho = 0.285$, $p = 0.050$), but this
did not reach statistical significance. Age showed no significant correlation with total
vertex-based scar burden (Spearman $\rho = -0.220$, $p = 0.132$, $n = 48$).

Sex-stratified analysis was not performed: only 10 female patients among the 48 patients
with available age data, against 38 male patients. This degree of imbalance renders any
formal comparison uninformative, and the predominantly male composition reflects the
known epidemiology of myocardial infarction---a real data limitation.

\subsection{Transmurality}

Figures~\ref{fig:transmurality_ring} and~\ref{fig:transmurality_territory} show
fragment-level onset and transmurality stratified by ring zone and coronary territory
respectively. Onset measures the shallowest wall depth at which scar begins (0 =
endocardial, 1 = epicardial); transmurality is the span from onset to outermost depth. Both
are derived from the continuous vertex-depth computation described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:statistical}. Interpretation of these patterns is provided in
Section~\ref{sec:transmurality_discussion}.

The continuous vertex-depth approach yields a mean fragment onset of 0.300 and a mean
fragment transmurality of 0.190, indicating that scar originates near the endocardial surface
and extends approximately 19\% of wall thickness---consistent with subendocardial ischaemic
infarction. Onset is lowest in the apical and mid rings and highest in the basal ring, which is
the most distant from the occlusion site. By coronary territory, RCA presents the highest
onset and the highest transmurality, making it the most deeply penetrating territory in this
cohort.

The transmurality gradient analysis shows a positive population-mean core-to-edge
difference, confirming a dense-core / thin-edge transmural structure: the central scar mass is
thicker in the wall-depth dimension than its periphery.

Endocardial dominance was confirmed at the cohort level: the large majority of scar-positive
bins showed onset $\leq 30\%$ of wall thickness. Midwall onset was present in a minority of
bins; epicardial-dominant onset was uncommon.

\subsection{Geometric descriptors and fragment shape}

The apex-to-base scar gradient showed near-zero population means (linear slope:
$-0.031 \pm 0.165$; Spearman $\rho$: $+0.088 \pm 0.425$) with extreme inter-patient
variability: slopes ranged from $-0.82$ (strongly apex-dominant) to $+0.20$ (base-dominant),
and $\rho$ from $-0.89$ to $+0.74$. No population-level longitudinal preference exists, but
the gradient is a highly informative individual descriptor not encoded in segment-level burden
or territory assignment alone.

Shape analysis of the largest scar fragment showed that most patients present irregular,
non-compact lesions (compactness $< 0.4$), with higher total scar burden associated with
wider circumferential spread and lower aspect ratio---suggesting that extensive infarcts grow
preferentially around the ventricular wall rather than radially through it. The mid ring is where
scar appears most circumferentially spread.

SVD analysis of the full vector showed that AHA segment burden features account for the
largest share of variance: 16 principal components are required to explain 90\% of the total
variance, compared to 11 for the segment-free variant, reflecting the higher redundancy
introduced by the 17 correlated segment columns. SVD contribution analysis identified ten
features with contributions below the 25th percentile---including global CZ burden, number
of involved territories, circumferential extent at mid and apical rings, compactness,
apex-to-base gradient, fragment count, and third and fourth fragment depth descriptors---which
were dropped to produce the pruned variants.

Contrary to the expectation that removing low-contribution features would reduce noise and
improve retrieval, pruning degraded performance on two of the three metrics: mean scar
burden error increased from 0.015 to 0.019 and mean segment Dice fell from 0.597 to 0.545,
with only a marginal gain in polar-map Dice (0.106 to 0.109). The same pattern held for the
segment-free variants: full\_ns\_pruned performed worse than full\_no\_seg on all three
metrics (scar error 0.019 vs 0.013; segment Dice 0.474 vs 0.489; polar Dice 0.093 vs
0.099). The SVD contribution threshold therefore does not identify genuinely redundant
features in this cohort---the dropped features, despite low individual variance contributions,
carry complementary discriminative information that improves retrieval when retained.

PC1 vs PC2 scatter plots of the $z$-score normalised vectors showed that all structured
vectors except full\_ns\_pruned produce a PC space where patients cluster coherently by CZ
burden, with higher-burden cases separating progressively along PC1. full\_ns\_pruned was
the only exception, producing a disorganised scatter with no clear burden gradient,
consistent with its inferior retrieval performance and suggesting that its 16 retained features
are insufficient to capture the dominant axis of scar variation in this cohort.

\textbf{pmap\_only} achieved the highest mean polar-map Dice (0.214) among all vectors but
the lowest segment Dice (0.359) and tied-worst scar burden error (0.019), reflecting the fact
that polar-map PCA scores encode spatial configuration but carry limited information about
total scar amount. The \textbf{full\_ns+pmap} hybrid achieved the second-lowest scar burden
error (0.014) and intermediate performance on both Dice metrics (segment Dice 0.484, polar
Dice 0.155), but did not outperform the full vector on any individual metric.

The full vector was selected as the primary descriptor for all subsequent analyses, as it
achieved the best segment Dice (0.597) and the lowest scar burden error among vectors
that also maintain reasonable polar-map overlap.

\subsection{Distance metric comparison}

Four distance metrics were evaluated on the full vector under leave-one-out conditions.
Weighted Euclidean and standard Euclidean produced identical results (mean
$|\Delta\text{CZ}| = 0.016$, mean segment Dice $= 0.539$, mean polar-map Dice $= 0.095$),
as expected given that both reduce to the same computation on $z$-score normalised
features. Manhattan distance outperformed both across all three measures (mean
$|\Delta\text{CZ}| = 0.015$, mean segment Dice $= 0.597$, mean polar-map Dice $= 0.106$).
Cosine distance performed substantially worse (mean $|\Delta\text{CZ}| = 0.044$, mean
segment Dice $= 0.282$, mean polar-map Dice $= 0.042$), consistent with its insensitivity to
vector magnitude: patients with similar scar geometry but very different total burdens appear
artificially close under cosine distance.

The superior performance of Manhattan distance is interpretable in this setting: because it
aggregates per-dimension differences linearly rather than quadratically, it is less sensitive to
large mismatches on individual features and better able to identify patients that are broadly
similar across the full descriptor. Manhattan distance on $z$-score normalised features was
adopted as the primary metric for all remaining analyses.

Figure~\ref{fig:deterministic} shows illustrative examples of deterministic retrieval under

\subsection{Probabilistic retrieval}

In practice, the softmax weights are strongly concentrated on the nearest neighbour when its
distance is substantially smaller than the second-nearest. This was observed for several
patients in the cohort, where the nearest neighbour received sampling probability close to
1.0, making the probabilistic and deterministic modes effectively equivalent for those cases.
For patients whose top-10 neighbours are more evenly spaced in descriptor space, the
probabilistic mode distributes weight across multiple candidates, allowing different plausible
scar patterns to be returned for the same query.

A quantitative comparison across all 105 patients showed that the deterministic mode
achieves marginally better mean retrieval quality on all three metrics ($|\Delta\text{CZ}| =
0.015$, segment Dice $= 0.597$, polar-map Dice $= 0.106$) compared to probabilistic
sampling ($|\Delta\text{CZ}| = 0.016$, segment Dice $= 0.570$, polar-map Dice $= 0.104$).
The small performance gap confirms that the deterministic nearest neighbour remains the
best single match, while the probabilistic mode provides a mechanism for generating a
distribution of plausible alternatives reflecting genuine population-level variability among
patients with similar scar profiles.

\subsection{Leave-one-out validation: population-level statistics}

The full leave-one-out evaluation was performed across all 105 patients using the full vector
and Manhattan distance. Summary statistics are reported in Table~\ref{tab:loostats} and the
metric distributions are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:loostats}.

The distribution of $|\Delta\text{CZ}|$ is right-skewed, with most retrievals showing small
discrepancies between target and retrieved scar amount. The mean absolute error was 0.015
(SD 0.019) and the median was 0.009, indicating that the central tendency of the cohort is
well matched. The right tail---cases with errors up to 0.106---corresponds to patients at the
extremes of the scar burden distribution, where few sufficiently similar patients exist in the
remaining 104.

The segment Dice had a mean of 0.597 (SD 0.314) and a median of 0.667, indicating that
on average two thirds of the target's involved segments are correctly identified by the
nearest neighbour. The interquartile range [0.500, 0.800] shows that the majority of
retrievals achieve segment-level spatial overlap above chance. Cases with low segment Dice
tend to correspond to multi-territory or diffuse scar patterns that are underrepresented in the
cohort.

The polar-map Dice had a mean of 0.106 (SD 0.145) and a median of 0.032. The lower
absolute values relative to segment Dice are expected: two patients can share the same set
of involved AHA segments while differing in the precise circumferential boundaries and
transmural depth of scar within those segments, and the polar-map Dice penalises any such
pixel-level misalignment. The long right tail (maximum 0.529, 75th percentile 0.189) reflects
the subset of patients for whom a geometrically close match exists in the case base.
