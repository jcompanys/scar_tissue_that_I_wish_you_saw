\section*{Clinical motivation: post-infarction scar as an arrhythmogenic substrate}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Clinical motivation: post-infarction scar as an arrhythmogenic substrate}

Myocardial infarction (MI) produces permanent structural remodeling of the left ventricular
myocardium. Following the acute ischemic event, prolonged oxygen shortage causes
irreversible cardiomyocyte necrosis. Because adult myocardial tissue has limited
regenerative capacity, the damaged region is progressively replaced by fibrotic scar.
Although this reparative process preserves the mechanical integrity of the ventricular wall, it
also creates a heterogeneous electrical substrate that can alter impulse propagation and
promote ventricular arrhythmias.

In chronic post-MI patients, ventricular tachycardia (VT) is commonly sustained by re-entrant
circuits associated with the infarct scar. Dense fibrotic tissue, usually referred to as the core
zone, is largely unexcitable. In contrast, the surrounding peri-infarct or border zone contains
a mixture of surviving myocytes, collagen deposition (accumulation of fibrous connective
tissue between cells), and altered cell-to-cell coupling (disrupted electrical connections
between cardiomyocytes due to gap-junction remodelling). This
heterogeneous tissue organization can generate slow conduction, conduction block, and
spatial dispersion of refractoriness, which together create the conditions required for
re-entry.

Importantly, the arrhythmogenic potential of post-infarction scar is not determined only by its
total amount. Two patients with similar scar burden may present different arrhythmic
vulnerability depending on scar location, transmural extent, compactness, and internal
organization. Narrow conducting corridors within or around scar tissue can act as protected
pathways for re-entry and may correspond to critical VT isthmuses. Therefore, scar should
not be understood only as a scalar biomarker, but as a spatially organized three-dimensional
structure embedded within the left ventricle.

Despite this mechanistic understanding, clinical risk stratification after MI remains limited.
Current strategies rely heavily on global functional markers, particularly left ventricular
ejection fraction (LVEF), the fraction of blood pumped out of the left ventricle with each
heartbeat. Although reduced LVEF is associated with increased arrhythmic
risk, it does not directly describe the substrate responsible for re-entry. Many patients with
severely reduced LVEF never develop malignant ventricular arrhythmias, while sudden
cardiac death may also occur in patients with moderately reduced or preserved LVEF. These
limitations motivate approaches that characterize the structure of scar more directly, with
particular attention to its geometry.

\section*{Imaging-based characterization of scar geometry}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Imaging-based characterization of scar geometry}

Non-invasive cardiac imaging plays a central role in the characterization of post-infarction
scar. Late gadolinium enhancement cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (LGE-CMR) is
widely considered the reference modality for in vivo visualization of myocardial fibrosis. After
gadolinium administration, fibrotic and infarcted tissue shows delayed contrast wash-out
compared with healthy myocardium, producing hyperenhanced regions that can be identified
as scar. This makes LGE-CMR particularly suitable for detecting post-infarction scar,
distinguishing dense core tissue from heterogeneous peri-infarct regions, and assessing the
transmural extent of fibrosis.

LGE-CMR provides the most complete characterization of myocardial scar because it
directly exploits contrast enhancement associated with fibrotic tissue. However, it also
presents technical challenges: cardiac and respiratory motion must be controlled, image
quality may vary across centres, and scar segmentation depends on intensity thresholds,
making border-zone delineation sensitive to acquisition parameters and observer decisions.
Even with these limitations, LGE-CMR remains the most appropriate imaging modality for
this thesis because the objective is to analyze scar geometry itself. Platforms such as ADAS
3D allow LGE-CMR data to be transformed into patient-specific three-dimensional
representations of the left ventricle, core zone, border zone, and slow-conduction channels.

Computed tomography has emerged as a complementary modality. Although CT offers faster acquisition, it lacks direct sensitivity
to fibrosis and therefore provides less specific scar characterization than LGE-CMR. Its
superior spatial resolution, however, enables detailed assessment of ventricular wall
thinning---a hallmark of chronic infarction---and recent work has shown that CT-derived wall
thickness heterogeneity can identify channels of relatively preserved thickness within thinned
regions that colocalize with VT isthmuses \cite{cedilnik2023}. Nevertheless, because this
thesis focuses on scar geometric characterization, LGE-CMR remains the primary data
source.

\section*{Standardized polar maps for population-level scar comparison}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Standardized polar maps for population-level scar comparison}

A major challenge in scar geometry analysis is that scar is patient-specific. Each scar
occupies a different location, follows the curvature of a different ventricular anatomy, and
may extend through the wall with variable transmurality. Direct comparison of
three-dimensional scar surfaces across patients is therefore difficult. The same apparent
shape may have different anatomical meaning depending on whether it is located at the
apex, mid-ventricle, or base.

To enable population-level analysis, patient-specific scar distributions must be mapped into a
common anatomical reference. The American Heart Association (AHA) 17-segment model \cite{cerqueira2002}
provides a standardized division of the left ventricle into basal, mid-cavity, apical, and
apical-cap regions. This standardization naturally supports the use of polar or bull's-eye
maps, where the longitudinal coordinate encodes apex-to-base position and the angular
coordinate encodes circumferential location. When aligned with the AHA model, polar maps
allow scar location and extent to be compared across patients in an anatomically meaningful
way, preserving clinically meaningful information about scar location, circumferential
extension, apex-to-base distribution, and transmurality.

\section*{Data curation and the need for scalable pipelines}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Data curation and the need for scalable pipelines}

Population-level scar geometry analysis requires patient-specific data to be collected,
organised, curated, and transformed into a consistent computational format. This thesis
was developed in collaboration with Centro M\'{e}dico Teknon using data from the
DEVELOP-VT dataset. A practical part of the project involved working directly with the
clinical and image-processing workflow at Teknon. This included reviewing available cases,
assisting with the segmentation of new cases, centralising the dataset, and extracting the
necessary files for each patient. Because ADAS~3D exports are case-specific and not
designed for large-scale batch extraction, this required substantial manual and
semi-automatic organisation before any population-level analysis could be performed.
More broadly, scalable computational cardiology increasingly requires automated pipelines;
the goal of this thesis is therefore to build a reproducible Python pipeline that transforms
patient-specific three-dimensional scar segmentations into standardised polar-map
representations suitable for cohort-level geometric analysis.

\section*{From scalar scar biomarkers to population-level geometry}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{From scalar scar biomarkers to population-level geometry}

Existing imaging-based approaches often summarize scar using scalar or regional
descriptors such as total scar mass, core mass, border-zone mass, or percentage of left
ventricular involvement. These variables are clinically useful but compress a complex
three-dimensional structure into a small set of numbers, losing information about scar
location, shape, spatial extension, transmurality, and relationship with anatomical regions.

Patient-specific simulation studies incorporate scar geometry but do not describe how it
varies across a population. Machine learning and generative imaging methods such as
CLAIM \cite{ramzan2025} and LGESynthNet \cite{jacob2024} have been proposed for scar
synthesis; while CLAIM uses a three-dimensional template to guide anatomically plausible scar
mask generation, both methods operate at the two-dimensional image and segmentation-mask level,
targeting image augmentation and segmentation performance rather than explicit population-level
geometric characterisation of scar shape. In contrast, this thesis analyzes the scar itself as a geometric object defined
on three-dimensional meshes, emphasizing shape properties---such as extent and
transmurality---over pixel-level position in imaging slices. Although the present work
prioritizes geometry, scar position within the ventricle remains important because the
underlying tissue architecture (fiber orientation, wall thickness, proximity to conduction
system) varies spatially and modulates arrhythmogenic behavior. Complementary work in
vascular modeling has demonstrated that coordinate-system-based geometric
representations enable quantitative point-wise comparison across patients \cite{romero2025},
supporting the broader hypothesis that clinically relevant information is contained in
spatial geometry beyond global scalar biomarkers \cite{romero2024}. Patient-specific
electrophysiological simulations using the same DEVELOP registry data have demonstrated
that integrating imaging with computational models of slow conduction channels enables
automated VT risk stratification in clinical timeframes \cite{serra2024}. Scalable automated
pipelines for generating large virtual patient cohorts confirm the feasibility of
population-scale cardiac modelling \cite{doste2026}.

\section*{Research gap and thesis rationale}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Research gap and thesis rationale}

Despite advances in imaging, simulation, and data-driven modelling, there remains a need
for reproducible frameworks that analyse post-infarction scar geometry at the population
level. Current approaches focus either on scalar biomarkers, patient-specific simulations, or
image-level scar synthesis. What is less developed is a standardised representation that
allows scar distributions from many patients to be compared directly while preserving
anatomical context.

Three research questions guide this thesis. \textbf{RQ1:} Is there a low-dimensional space
that can reproduce plausible post-infarction scar geometries? \textbf{RQ2:} Can scar
geometry be described by a known parametric distribution? \textbf{RQ3:} How can
post-infarction scar geometry be parameterised in a compact representation that preserves
clinically meaningful structure?

\section*{Objectives}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Objectives}

The main objective of this thesis is to develop and apply a reproducible computational
pipeline for the geometric characterisation of post-infarction scar across a patient cohort.
Specifically, the work aims to curate and organise the DEVELOP-VT dataset, including
patient-specific ADAS~3D outputs and VTK mesh files, and to design and implement a
Python pipeline for processing three-dimensional scar segmentations obtained from
LGE-CMR data. It further aims to project scar distributions onto standardised
two-dimensional left ventricular polar maps aligned with the AHA 17-segment model,
enabling cross-patient comparison of scar location, shape, and transmurality in a common
anatomical reference frame. The pipeline is applied to the DEVELOP-VT cohort, comprising
168 post-MI patients of whom 105 presented usable core-zone segmentations, to perform
population-level analysis of scar geometry, assess how scar location conditions apparent
scar shape and spatial organisation, and determine the intrinsic dimensionality of the
scar-pattern space. Finally, the work proposes and evaluates a retrieval-based generative
framework that exploits the standardised polar-map representation to produce spatially
coherent synthetic scar cases from existing population patterns.

\section*{Main Contributions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Main Contributions}

The DEVELOP-VT clinical dataset was curated and organised, centralising records
distributed across five acquisition years. Approximately 40 additional cases were
segmented at Centro M\'{e}dico Teknon as part of this work, expanding the usable cohort.
A fully automated, reproducible polar-map pipeline was implemented for cohort-scale scar
projection, enabling population-level geometric characterisation of post-infarction scar
anatomy. A retrieval-based generation baseline was proposed and evaluated, preserving
realistic scar patterns and providing a foundation for future simulation or data-augmentation
studies.
